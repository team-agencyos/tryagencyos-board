The eight phases

step by step

Phases 0–2 are strictly sequential. Phases 3, 4 and 6 run as parallel tracks with separate owners. No phase starts until the previous one's done when is actually true. Open any phase for its steps.

Inventory & decisionsPhase 0 · Weeks 1–3IN PROGRESS

Before you move anything, you need to know what exists. Most merger pain comes from an account nobody remembered until it broke.

  1. Build the master register. One row per account. It is the single source of truth for the whole migration and stays live until Phase 8.
  2. Sweep both estates. Password manager exports, Workspace admin user and app lists, 24 months of card statements (this finds the subscriptions nobody remembers), OAuth grants, every client folder.
  3. Flag single-point-of-failure accounts. Any account where one person can log in, or the recovery email is personal, or 2FA sits on one phone.
  4. Map client → platforms. Every platform we touch and which identity holds it. This becomes the Phase 5 queue.
  5. Record verification dependencies. Which Search Console properties are verified by which method, which domains verified in which Business Manager, which Ads accounts linked to which manager.
  6. Make the seven decisions and write the answers down.
Done whenEvery account appears in the register with an owner and a migration method, the seven decisions are recorded, and the SPOF list has been reviewed.
FoundationPhase 1 · Weeks 3–5PARTLY DONE

Stand up the destination properly. Everything after this depends on the new domain being a real, trusted, well-secured identity — not one someone bought last week.

  1. Consolidate the registrar. All domains under one company-owned account. Registrar lock and auto-renew on. Admin contact is a role address two people can read.
  2. Attach the domain to the surviving tenant. If it sits in the other tenant, remove it there first — a domain cannot exist in two tenants at once.
  3. Configure mail authentication before sending anything. SPF, DKIM per sending source, DMARC at p=none with a report address. Review two weeks, fix failures, then step to quarantine.
  4. Start deliverability warm-up now. A cold domain emailing 200 clients lands in spam, or reads as phishing. Begin low-volume sending in week 3 so there is 4–6 weeks of reputation before clients see it in Phase 5.
  5. One password manager, one vault structure. Shared vault per client plus internal. Merge every estate's vault, deduplicate, rotate anything ever shared by export or chat.
  6. Security baseline. Enforced 2FA, hardware keys or an app (not SMS) for admins, recovery codes in the vault with at least two named holders, documented break-glass account.
  7. Naming convention. Personal, role and client-access addresses decided and written down.
Done whenDMARC reports clean, 2FA enforced for everyone, credentials in one vault, and the domain has been sending real mail for two weeks.
Identity & mailPhase 2 · Weeks 5–9NOT STARTED

Get every human onto the new address without losing a single inbound message, calendar invite, or thread of history.

  1. Create new addresses as additions. Everyone gets the new address while the old one still works normally.
  2. Alias, don't redirect. Attach legacy domains as secondary domains so every old address becomes an alias into the same mailbox. Nothing bounces.
  3. Migrate the absorbed tenants' mailboxes. Note the plural — confirm how many tenants actually exist first. Run one pilot user end to end before touching the rest.
  4. Migrate shared Drive content into company-owned shared drives. This is the moment to fix files-owned-by-whoever-made-them, permanently.
  5. Rebuild recurring calendar invites. Editing the organiser on a live series is unreliable and silently drops external guests.
  6. Switch default send-from for everyone on the same day. Keep the old address as a send-as option for existing threads.
  7. One signature, applied centrally. Draft the legacy auto-replies now but leave them off until Phase 7.
Done whenEveryone has worked from the new address for 10 straight business days, nothing has bounced, and a test to each retired address arrives in the right mailbox.
Internal operationsPhase 3 · Weeks 8–12NOT STARTED

One place to talk, one place to plan, one place to track clients. Where the companies actually start behaving as one.

  1. Slack. Pick the surviving workspace, export the other for archive, recreate channels under one convention, re-establish client Connect channels.
  2. Docs. One workspace. Import the other side into a clearly marked Legacy section rather than interleaving on day one. Reorganise after, not during.
  3. Project management. Migrate active projects only. Archive closed work in place and link to it.
  4. CRM. Deduplicate companies and people before importing, agree one pipeline definition, and keep which estate each relationship came from as a field.
  5. Reconnect integrations under the new identity. Anything authorised with an old address dies silently when that account retires.
  6. Merge the SOPs. Where both had a process, pick one. Where one did, adopt it.
Done whenExactly one place for each function, all client Connect channels live in the surviving workspace, no duplicate companies in the CRM.
Engineering estatePhase 4 · Weeks 10–14PARTLY DONE

One GitHub org, one Vercel team, one set of secrets. Expect breakage — rehearse it on something unimportant.

  1. Choose the surviving org and set up teams that mirror how you actually work before moving anything in.
  2. Transfer repos one at a time, starting with an unimportant one. Transfer carries issues, PRs and history. Expect to rebuild branch protection, Actions secrets and variables, environments, deploy keys, webhooks, and app installations. Write down what actually broke — that list is the checklist for the rest.
  3. Fix references after each transfer. Redirects work but are not a foundation: remotes, submodules, registry paths, image references, docs links, any CI config naming the old org.
  4. Consolidate hosting. Transfer projects, then confirm environment variables, custom domains and the Git integration on each. Lower DNS TTLs 24–48h before moving a production domain; move one low-traffic project first.
  5. Rotate every secret the retiring estate could see. A merger is the right moment; it will never be cheaper.
  6. Re-point CI/CD, monitoring and alerting — then confirm a test alert actually arrives.
Done whenEvery active repo builds and deploys from the new org, no pipeline references the old one, and all exposed secrets are rotated.
Client platform accessPhase 5 · Weeks 12–24NOT STARTED

The phase that can cost you a client. One client per week at most, and always talk to them before touching their accounts.

  1. Sequence by risk, lowest first. Start small and forgiving. Learn what breaks. Work up to the largest accounts last.
  2. Announce before you touch anything, from the old address, signed by a person they know, naming exactly which addresses and account IDs will contact them. Skip this and your access requests are indistinguishable from an attack.
  3. Get written acknowledgement that the new address is the official contact. One line in a reply is enough.
  4. Work the platform playbooks for that one client — add everywhere before removing anything.
  5. Dual-run 60–90 days with both identities live, working exclusively from the new one so gaps surface while the net is still there.
  6. Remove old access, confirm removal by attempting it, and mark the client complete.
Done whenEvery client has completed the runbook, dual-run has elapsed, and no client work has depended on a legacy identity for 30 days.
Vendors & billingPhase 6 · Weeks 16–20NOT STARTED

Two companies means two of most things. This phase usually pays for the whole migration.

  1. List every recurring charge from 24 months of statements on both sides. Anything charging you that isn't in the register is forgotten or unowned.
  2. Deduplicate. Pick one, migrate the data, cancel the other at renewal rather than mid-term.
  3. Re-point billing contacts and payment methods to the surviving entity and a role address.
  4. Move account ownership off individuals. Vendor accounts on a personal address are the most common thing to break when someone leaves.
  5. Consolidate accounting, invoicing and payroll under the surviving entity.
  6. Update insurance, certifications and partner statuses to the new entity and domain.
Done whenOne subscription per function, no charges to an unowned account, one entity invoicing from one system.
Brand & external presencePhase 7 · Weeks 20–24NOT STARTED

Now the plumbing works, make the outside world consistent. Earlier creates a public brand the internal systems can't back up.

  1. Website on the new domain, with 301s from legacy domains page by page — not a blanket redirect to the homepage. Legacy domains keep resolving indefinitely.
  2. Social, directories and listings — company pages, business profiles, directories, partner listings.
  3. Case studies and collateral rebranded, with a line acknowledging work delivered under the legacy names. It preserves the credibility rather than erasing it.
  4. Turn on the legacy auto-replies drafted in Phase 2.
  5. Public announcement — only after every client has heard directly. Clients should never learn about their agency's merger from social media.
Done whenEvery public surface carries one brand, legacy URLs redirect to real equivalents, inbound leads land at the new address.
Decommission & archivePhase 8 · Week 36+NOT STARTED

The temptation is to do this early to save money. Don't. Keeping things alive is trivially cheap against one unrecoverable account.

  1. Freeze, don't delete. Retired mailboxes become archived or suspended accounts with forwarding intact, for at least a year.
  2. Export before shutting anything down — Slack, PM tool, CRM, Drive — into one documented archive location.
  3. Keep legacy domains renewed permanently. An expired domain that once received client mail is a genuine security problem.
  4. Close the retiring tenant only after data is exported, verified readable, and nothing forwards through it.
  5. Final access audit. No legacy identity retains access anywhere, every account has a named owner.
  6. Retire the register into a quarterly access review. This is the lasting value of the whole exercise.
Done whenEvery register row reads Retired, exports are verified readable, and a quarterly access review is scheduled with an owner.

Playbooks

how, not what

The phases say what moves. These say how — the actual steps, who has to click, and the specific ways each platform goes wrong. Read the general model once; work the rest per client.

The general modelRead this first
Who can act: Everyone, once.
  1. Work out which of three shapes the access is. Agency link — our organisation is linked to their asset (Ads manager, Meta Partner, Shopify collaborator); both old and new can coexist, client approves. Named-user grant — an email holds a role (GA4, Search Console, GTM, CMS); we self-serve if we hold admin. Ownership — the asset belongs to an account (domains, pixels, GA accounts, repos); that's title, not access.
  2. Hunt for ownership dressed up as access. A client's pixel inside our Business Manager, their GA property inside our Analytics account, their domain inside our registrar login. These look like access and are actually us holding their asset. Find them in Phase 0, not on migration day.
  3. Establish who can act. We hold admin → self-serve. Client holds admin → they must click, so make the ask unmistakably legitimate. Nobody's sure → stop and find out; this is the state that produces lockouts.
  4. Then the four beats. Add alongside → verify by doing real work → dual-run 60–90 days → retire and confirm removal.
  5. Order within a client, lowest consequence first: read-only and reporting → analytics and measurement (breaks visibility, recoverable) → advertising and spend (breaks money, live) → production and publishing (breaks their site) → commercial and billing (breaks getting paid). Never start at the bottom because it looks administrative.
How it goes wrong
  • An accepted invite proves nothing — permissions are frequently granted at the wrong level and look fine until someone tries to work.
Domains & DNSMost destructive
Who can act: Depends who owns the domain. Classify every one before touching any.
  1. Classify first. Agency-owned → consolidate into one company account. Client-owned but we hold the login → that's ownership, decide with the client. Client-owned and they hold it → document who to reach urgently.
  2. Understand the split: registration is who the domain is rented from; DNS hosting is which nameservers answer for it. Transferring registration does not move the zone. Move DNS first, registration second, never the same day.
  3. Export the complete current zone — A, AAAA, CNAME, MX, TXT, SRV, CAA. This is your rollback.
  4. Work out what every TXT record does before assuming any is stale. They hold SPF, DKIM, DMARC, site verification and sending-domain proofs. Deleting one 'unused' record can unverify Search Console weeks later with no obvious cause.
  5. Recreate the zone at the new provider exactly, record for record. Don't tidy it yet.
  6. Lower TTL to 300s and wait out the old TTL. If it was 24 hours, you wait 24 hours. This is what makes the switch reversible in minutes.
  7. Query the new nameservers directly before switching — especially MX.
  8. Switch nameservers, then watch 24–48 hours. Site loads, mail delivers both ways, DNS-based verifications still read verified. Raise TTLs once stable.
  9. Only then transfer registration. Check the 60-day locks, unlock, disable privacy temporarily, confirm the registrant email is one you can read, get the auth code, initiate at the gaining registrar, approve, then re-lock and set auto-renew.
How it goes wrong
  • Email dies silently — nameservers changed without MX copied. Nobody notices for hours.
  • Site down a full day — TTL was 24h and the switch was wrong.
  • A platform quietly unverifies — a TXT record wasn't carried over.
  • Transfer approval never arrives — registrant email is unread or privacy-proxied.
  • Domain expires mid-migration — auto-renew was on the old account's card.
Google Search ConsoleHighest risk
Who can act: Us, if our identity is a verified owner. Otherwise the client adds us first.
  1. Understand the two layers. A verified owner proved control through a method — DNS TXT, HTML file, meta tag, an Analytics link or a Tag Manager link. Everyone else was granted access by a verified owner and hangs off it. The question is never who has access, it's whose verification is holding the property up.
  2. Know that verification chains into other systems. Verified via Analytics → removing that GA access unverifies Search Console. Via Tag Manager → same. Via an HTML file → a site rebuild kills it. Via DNS TXT → survives all of it. That's why DNS is the answer.
  3. Before anything: open Settings → Ownership verification on every property. Record who the verified owners are and which method each used, and note Domain properties versus URL-prefix properties. Clients usually have several accumulated over the years.
  4. Create or claim a Domain property for the root domain under the new identity and verify it with a DNS TXT record — added in addition to existing verification records, never replacing one. This is the durable anchor: independent of the site, of GA, of GTM, of any other user.
  5. Add the new identity as owner on every existing URL-prefix property too, so historical data stays reachable at the granularity their reports use.
  6. Verify by doing real work — pull a performance report, submit a sitemap, run a URL inspection.
  7. Dual-run. Search Console data is property-level, not user-level, so nothing is lost by both identities being present. There is no cost to patience here.
  8. Remove the old identity last, one property at a time, re-checking verification after each removal.
How it goes wrong
  • Everyone loses access at once — the only verified owner was removed and all delegated owners cascaded off.
  • Unverified weeks later — verification ran through a GA or GTM link that a different playbook removed.
  • Unverified after a site launch — HTML-file verification, and the rebuild dropped the file.
  • New property with no history — someone created a replacement instead of adding a user. Data does not backfill.
  • Only the www property moved — the others were never listed.
Google Analytics (GA4)Ownership question
Who can act: Us, if we hold Administrator. Editor is not enough to manage users.
  1. Answer the ownership question first: whose Analytics account is the data in? If the property sits in an agency account, the client's history is ours, not theirs — that's ownership, and it's live in a merger because 'which agency account' is exactly what's changing. If it sits in a personal Google account, fix it immediately; it leaves with the person.
  2. Check whether admin is held at account or property level. They're separate screens and it's easy to grant the wrong one.
  3. List every property in scope — clients often have a legacy Universal property and test properties alongside GA4.
  4. Note existing product links — Ads, Search Console, BigQuery export, Merchant Center. These depend on the linking user having rights in both products.
  5. Admin → Account (or Property) access management → add the new identity as Administrator, at the level matching the old identity so you don't quietly reduce scope.
  6. Verify by opening the Admin section, not just a report. Seeing data but not Admin means the role is wrong.
  7. Re-check product links after adding — an Ads link created by a user who later loses Ads access can stop passing data.
  8. Dual-run, then remove the old identity. GA4 refuses to remove the last Administrator — treat a refusal as a signal that your new grant didn't apply where you thought.
How it goes wrong
  • Can see reports, can't administer — granted at property level when the old access was account level, or Editor instead of Administrator.
  • Ads ↔ GA4 link stops passing conversions — tied to a user who lost Ads access.
  • A property gets missed — usually a legacy one still referenced in reports.
  • BigQuery export breaks — the linking user or service account changed.
Google AdsConversion trap
Who can act: Client must approve the new manager link. We can only send the request.
  1. Check conversion action ownership before anything else. Goals → Conversions → Summary, and look at the owning account of each action. Agencies often define conversions at manager level and share them down. If the client's conversions belong to the old manager account, unlinking it stops conversions recording — Smart Bidding then optimises against nothing and performance degrades over days rather than failing loudly.
  2. Check what else lives at manager level — shared budgets, negative keyword lists, audiences, scripts.
  3. Get the 10-digit customer ID and list current manager links under Admin → Access and security → Managers.
  4. Check billing. If we're billed and rebill the client, changing entity affects the payment profile and possibly tax details. If the client is billed directly, this is much simpler.
  5. Send the link request from the surviving manager account — and tell the client it's coming, naming the manager ID so they can match it. An unexplained manager request is indistinguishable from an account takeover, and Google warns users about exactly this.
  6. Client approves, then verify with a real reversible edit — a bid modifier, pause and unpause an ad group, add a negative keyword. Reading isn't proof of edit rights.
  7. Leave both managers linked through the dual-run. Ads supports multiple managers on one account — that is the safety net.
  8. Migrate anything the old manager owned and confirm the client account is recording conversions on the new setup across at least a week of real traffic.
  9. Remove the old manager link last.
How it goes wrong
  • Performance decays over a week — conversions belonged to the old manager account and stopped recording on unlink.
  • Client refuses the request — it arrived unannounced and looked like a scam. Correctly refused.
  • Campaigns pause on billing failure — payment profile tied to the retiring entity.
  • Lost access entirely — old manager removed before the new one was approved.
Meta Business ManagerPer-asset
Who can act: Client adds our Business Manager as a Partner and assigns each asset.
  1. Use Partner access, business to business — not adding our people as users inside their Business Manager. Partner access is the only model that survives staff changes and a merger cleanly.
  2. Check pixel ownership first. If the pixel was created inside our Business Manager, the client's site is firing our pixel and the audience and conversion history belong to us. Pixel ownership is effectively not transferable — the practical remedy is a new dataset and re-tagging, which loses historical audience accumulation. Same question for the catalog and sometimes the Page.
  3. Send our Business Manager ID plus an explicit asset list. Never say 'the usual access' — list it.
  4. Client: Business Settings → Partners → Add Partner → enter our BM ID, then assign each asset individually.
  5. Assets are granted one by one, and each is a separate thing to forget: ad account, Facebook Page, Instagram account, pixel/dataset, catalog, WhatsApp, domain verification. The pixel and catalog are the ones most often missed.
  6. Verify every asset individually from our side — open the ad account and make a reversible edit, open Events Manager and confirm the pixel is receiving events, open the catalog and confirm items load. A Partner connection can exist with almost nothing shared through it.
  7. Check payment methods and System Users. A payment method on the retiring business stops spend when it lapses; System User tokens are tied to a business and break silently.
  8. Dual-run, then the client removes the old Partner.
How it goes wrong
  • Partner added, nothing works — the connection exists but assets were never assigned.
  • Retargeting audiences empty — the pixel belonged to the retiring business.
  • Shopping ads break — catalog never shared.
  • Reporting integration dies — System User token tied to the old business.
  • Client can't find the settings — Business Settings is genuinely hard to navigate. This is the platform most worth a 15-minute screen-share.
Tag Manager & Merchant CenterPublish rights
Who can act: Us, if we hold account-level user management.
  1. Tag Manager: account and container permissions are separate. Account access lets you see the container list; container access is what lets you work. Granting only account access produces someone who can see everything and change nothing.
  2. Publish rights are separate again. Roles run Read → Edit → Approve → Publish. Someone with Edit can build a change and not ship it — you'll find out during an urgent tracking fix.
  3. Verify by actually publishing a trivial change. It's the only proof that counts.
  4. Never create a new container. The container ID is embedded in the client's site; a new one means a code change and the loss of all version history.
  5. Check whether GTM verifies Search Console before removing GTM access — move Search Console to DNS verification first.
  6. Merchant Center: Settings → People and access, match the old role exactly.
  7. Verify the Ads link is still active and the feed still fetching — check last fetch time and error count after the change, then daily for a week.
  8. Check the website claim status, which often runs through the same Search Console verification chain. Sequence Search Console before Merchant Center.
How it goes wrong
  • Feed stops and listings drop — a fetch credential or claim tied to the retiring identity.
  • Shopping campaigns stop serving — the Ads link silently dropped.
  • Server-side containers overlooked — they have their own hosting and permissions.
Shopify, CMS, email & cloudThe long tail
Who can act: Mixed — Shopify needs client approval, most of the rest we self-serve.
  1. Shopify: request a collaborator account, not a staff account. Collaborators don't consume staff seats and are clearly labelled as agency access. Ask for the collaborator request code up front. Request only the scopes we need — over-asking gets declined.
  2. CMS: create a new Administrator user. Never rename or re-email the old user — that breaks authorship on existing content. Log in as the new user and confirm you can edit, publish and reach settings.
  3. Hosting is a separate login from the CMS. Add the new identity there too, and rotate SFTP and SSH keys — keys shared between two former companies should not survive the merger.
  4. Re-register paid plugin and theme licences. The one everyone misses: licences are tied to an email, and when that mailbox retires the site keeps working and quietly stops receiving security updates.
  5. Email platforms: verify the sending domain is still authenticated. The DKIM records live in the client's DNS and survive a user change — but not a careless DNS migration. Send a real test to a seed list before removing anything.
  6. CRM and SaaS seats are limited and paid. Ask before you're mid-migration. Reassign user-owned automations — sequences and workflows pause when their owner is deactivated.
  7. Cloud, repos and hosting that belong to the client: migrate our access, never move the asset into our org. Rotate every key the retiring estate could see, and confirm alerts actually arrive at the new address.
  8. Slack Connect: re-invite from the surviving workspace. History doesn't follow — export the old channel, archive it, and post a pointer in the new one.
  9. Recurring meetings: recreate the series from the new identity. Changing the organiser on a live series drops external guests without telling anyone. Confirm acceptance, then cancel the old.
How it goes wrong
  • Site silently stops getting security updates — plugin licences still on a retired mailbox.
  • Automations pause — a CRM workflow's owning user was deactivated rather than reassigned.
  • Alerts go nowhere — monitoring still points at a retired address, and nobody notices until an incident.
Talking to clientsDo this first
Who can act: The relationship lead, from the old address.
  1. Accept the core problem: everything we're about to do looks exactly like an account takeover. A new domain emails them, then asks for admin access to their advertising and analytics. A well-trained client should be suspicious. Our job is to make legitimacy obvious and repeated — and to be glad when they check.
  2. Announce from the old address. It's the trusted one; use it while we still have it. An announcement from the new domain proves nothing.
  3. A known human signs it — the person that client already talks to, in their normal voice. Not 'The Team'.
  4. Never batch-send. Twelve identical emails going out at once reads like a compromised mailbox. Send individually, spread across the sequence.
  5. T−14: the announcement. Answer their four real questions in order — is my service changing (no, say it first), who is my contact (same person, name them), what do I have to do (nothing yet, and I'll tell you exactly when), is my price or contract changing (answer honestly).
  6. T−7: the anti-phishing message. The exact account IDs they'll see, the exact addresses invites come from, a sentence saying we will never ask for a password or a code, a phone number, and an explicit invitation to call and check.
  7. T+0 onward: ping before each request. 'Sending the Meta partner request now — it'll come from BM ID 123456, approving takes a minute, here's where the button is.' Screenshot anything in Business Settings.
  8. Call the big ones instead of emailing. Fifteen minutes, and ask the question that's worth the call on its own: is there a time of year or a campaign window where you'd rather we didn't touch the accounts?
  9. If a client refuses or goes quiet: don't escalate through a new channel. Go back to the old address or the phone. Offer a screen-share and let them drive. Ask what would make them comfortable — it's usually smaller than you expect. Extend the dual-run; the old access still works and nothing is urgent.
How it goes wrong
  • Never email bank detail changes. Phone call on a number they already have, both directions. It's the single most impersonated event in B2B fraud and their finance team is right to block it.
  • Never let a client hear it publicly first. Clients hear directly, the public announcement comes second.
  • Never ask for a credential. Every request in this migration is a link approval or an invite acceptance.
